About the book

Some things you only learn about God in places you never wanted to be.

I did not write this book after the storm. I wrote it in the middle of it. In seasons when I had prayed, waited, and still could not see what God was doing, one question kept finding me: is God really who I have been saying He is?

Chapter One begins right there, at the moment the words we say about God stop being just words. It walks with Peter onto the sea, sits with one of the most honest prayers in Scripture, and asks a question many of us avoid: whose voice gets to define you? Because this book is about faith, but it is also about identity, and why the person you used to be is not the person you have to remain.

Before you begin

A Letter to You

By Chuks Okonkwo3 min read
CFrom the desk ofChuks OkonkwoOttawa, Canada

Dear reader,

Before anything else, thank you.

Out of everything competing for your attention today, you chose to open a book about faith. I do not take that lightly. Whoever you are, and wherever you are reading this from, I am really glad you are here.

Let me tell you why this book exists.

It did not start as a book. It started as prayers, questions, and lines I kept coming back to in seasons when I had prayed, waited, and still could not see what God was doing.

Seasons that stretched me and asked more of me than I thought I had. I would never have chosen them, and I would not trade them now.

Through all of it, one question kept following me.

Is God still who I say He is, even here?

This book is my honest answer.

Here is something I want you to know before you go any further. You are not walking into this alone. I know what it feels like to stand at the edge of something, heart racing, torn between staying where it feels safe and stepping toward the voice that is calling you.

So let us not treat this like an ordinary book. Let it be a walk we take together. I will share what I learned, where I fell, and the Scriptures that kept me standing. You bring whatever you are carrying: your questions, your doubts, your hopes, even your silence. There is room for all of it here.

Even when we cannot see His footsteps, God is still walking through the water. That is the promise I want to hold out to you.

I am not going to tell you what is waiting in these pages. Some things are better discovered than described. But I will tell you this much: I pray that by the last page, you see God, and maybe even yourself, a little differently.

I cannot wait for you to find out how.

Thank you for giving me your time. I promise to honour it. Now let us see what God does with it.

Turn the page. I will meet you on the water.

With gratitude,

Chuks Okonkwo

P.S. Keep this letter close. When you finish Chapter One, come back and read it again. I think it will sound different.

What Is Faith?

20 min read17 readings12 passages of Scripture

I have heard the word faith for as long as I can remember. I grew up in the Catholic Church, and later I found my way into Pentecostal churches. They were two very different rooms, with very different styles of worship, but one word kept showing up in both of them.

Faith.

I heard it in homilies and in sermons. In hymns and in worship songs. In prayers over meals and prayers over people.

“Have faith.”

“Just believe.”

“Your faith will carry you.”

And if I am honest, for a long time I thought I knew exactly what it meant.

Faith meant belief.

You believe God exists and that Jesus died and rose again. You hold the right beliefs, you say yes in your heart, and that is faith.

Simple.

But at some point, I started doing something I had not done enough of before. I stopped taking the word for granted and started taking it to the Scriptures. I wanted to know, not just what I had heard faith was, but what the Bible actually says it is.

So I read. I studied. I compared verses. I looked up the original words. I asked questions I had never asked out loud.

And the deeper I went, the clearer it became.

Faith is so much more than belief.

That discovery is what this book is about. Not a definition to memorise, but a journey into what faith really is, and what it looks like when life puts it to the test.

So let us begin where it begins for most of us.

There is a moment that comes to almost everyone who genuinely decides to trust God.

A moment when the words stop being words.

“God is faithful.” “God will provide.” “God knows what He is doing.” “I trust God.”

You have said them and heard them. You may have even encouraged someone else with them.

Then life asks you a question.

Not with a sermon or a Bible study, but with circumstances.

“Do you really believe what you have been saying?”

That question changes everything.

Because trusting God on a clear road takes very little. Try doing it when you cannot see the road at all.

When provision is sitting in your account, calling God your provider comes naturally. Having a need with no obvious source is another story.

Saying God is in control feels simple while life is moving according to your plans. Then the plans collapse, and He does not immediately explain why.

And we can all sing about surrender, until surrender actually costs us something.

I have learned that some of the deepest things God teaches us do not happen when everything is working.

They happen when something is not. After the prayer has been prayed and the answer has not come. While the door is still closed and the future is still blurry. When you have done everything you know to do, and the only thing left is to trust.

And perhaps that is where we need to begin.

Not with a complicated definition or religious language, but with a word so simple that we almost overlook its weight.

Trust.

Faith is trust.

Simple.

But not easy.

Because trust becomes weighty when you have to place something precious inside it.

When Belief Becomes Personal

The New Testament uses the Greek word pistis for faith.

It carries the ideas of faith, trust, confidence, conviction, reliance, and faithfulness.

And that gives us something important.

Biblical faith is not merely agreeing that God exists.

James 2:19 reminds us that even demons believe that God exists.

So the question is not simply:

“Do you believe in God?”

The deeper question is:

“Do you trust God?”

There is a difference.

You can believe a chair will hold you and still stand beside it. Trust is when you sit down.

Or think about a plane. Believing the pilot knows how to fly gets you nowhere if you refuse to board. Trust is taking your seat, buckling in, and letting the aircraft leave the ground.

At some point, belief has to become personal. What you say you believe has to become the ground you stand on.

And this is where the subject becomes uncomfortable.

Because trusting God means there will be moments when His Word has to become more authoritative to you than your circumstances.

Not because circumstances are imaginary.

They are real. The bill is real. So is the disappointment, the closed door, and the waiting.

But there is another reality that is greater than what your eyes can immediately measure.

God.

You Cannot Trust Who You Refuse to Know

Why should we trust God?

Christianity does not ask you to place your confidence in an invisible stranger.

God reveals Himself. He gives us His Word, shows us His character, and teaches us His ways.

Romans 10:17 says:

There is a reason Scripture connects trust with hearing.

You cannot deeply trust someone you refuse to know.

Think about the people you trust most.

You did not trust them because someone gave you a five-minute presentation about their character.

You trusted them because you watched them. Time revealed them. You saw how they behaved when circumstances changed, and whether their words carried weight. You remembered how they treated you when you were vulnerable.

And Scripture does the same thing with God.

Look at Abraham. Joseph. Moses. David. Ruth. Daniel. Peter. Paul.

Generation after generation, Scripture keeps revealing the character of God.

Then, if you pay attention, you begin to see Him in your own story.

Sometimes the testimony is not, “God gave me exactly what I wanted.” It is, “God kept me when I thought I would break.”

The miracle may not be that the storm disappeared. It may be that you came out of the storm still knowing His name.

There are things you only learn about God in places you never wanted to be.

And there is another thing you begin to learn there.

If you can trust God with your future, you have to learn to trust what God says about you.

Whose Voice Defines You?

This is where trust becomes deeply personal.

Because many people believe God can change their circumstances but struggle to believe what God says about their identity.

You trust God to provide money, but when He says you are valuable, you hesitate.

You are sure He can open a door. Yet when He calls you His workmanship, you doubt Him.

And you believe He answers prayer, but you still let somebody else’s words decide who you are.

And that is a dangerous place to live.

Because people can speak from their pain, their anger, or their disappointment. They speak from what they think they know about you, and sometimes from a version of you that no longer exists.

But God speaks from truth.

Not every voice that knows your name knows your identity.

I recently watched a video of a woman who was being spoken to harshly by her parents.

The words being thrown at her were painful.

Words that could easily become a prison if she accepted them.

But what caught my attention was her response.

As the negative words came, she answered them.

“I did not kill anybody. I did not steal. I am not useless. I will make it. I am not what you are calling me.”

She refused to let someone else’s words become her identity.

And that made me think about something much deeper.

What do you do when somebody speaks a lie about you?

Do you rehearse it? Internalize it? Carry it for ten years? Let one person’s opinion become the narrator of your life?

Or do you return to what God has said?

Because Scripture says something powerful.

You were made in the image of God.

Genesis 1:27 says:

Before anybody had an opinion about you, God had already placed His image upon you.

Long before somebody called you useless, God called His creation good.

And when someone told you that you would never become anything, God already had purpose in mind.

Ephesians 2:10 says:

You are God’s workmanship.

Not somebody else’s unfinished opinion, and not the worst thing anyone has ever said about you. Not even the worst thing you have ever done. Your rejection, your failure, your previous season and your greatest mistake do not get to name you.

God’s workmanship.

And if you are in Christ, there is another truth you cannot afford to forget.

The Person You Used to Be Is Not the Person You Have to Remain

I know what it feels like to have your past brought back to you.

There was a time when someone brought my past directly to my face.

And I remember my response.

I said, essentially:

“The person you are talking about has died.”

That person is in the burial ground.

You can look for him. Search the earth, the rooms where he used to live, the memories, the old mistakes.

But you will not find him here.

Because the person standing in front of you is a new creation in Christ.

2 Corinthians 5:17 says:

That is not motivational language. It is Scripture.

So let me introduce myself again.

My name is Chuks.

And I am in Christ.

Full stop.

The past can explain where you came from.

It does not have permission to decide where you are going.

That does not mean you deny what happened.

It means you refuse to make what happened your permanent identity.

There is a difference between remembering your past and living inside it.

Paul understood this.

He had persecuted the church and taken part in violence against believers. His past was not imaginary.

Yet God still called him.

The same man who once persecuted the church became one of the greatest proclaimers of the gospel.

Grace did not pretend Paul’s past never happened.

Grace gave his future a new meaning.

Your past may be part of your testimony without being the title of your life.

And this is why identity belongs inside our understanding of trust.

Whatever God says about you, trust Him. He calls you His own, His workmanship, a new creation, chosen and beloved. And for those who are in Christ Jesus, there is no condemnation.

Do not allow people to preach a different gospel over your life.

When the World Calls You Something Else

There will be voices.

Some will tell you that you are not enough, or remind you of what you did five years ago. Others will compare you with someone else, or say you are behind, too broken, or never going to become what you hope to become.

Those voices can come from strangers, from friends, even from family. And sometimes, the loudest voice will be your own.

But you have to learn something:

A repeated lie does not become truth simply because you have heard it for a long time.

So believe God when He calls you His workmanship. Take Him at His word that He has begun a good work in you, and that you are a new creation. And if He says He has plans for you, submit those plans to Him and keep walking.

Jeremiah 29:11 reminds us that God’s thoughts toward His people are thoughts of peace and a future and hope.

Ephesians 1 speaks of being blessed in Christ and chosen in Him.

1 Peter 2:9 calls God’s people:

Look at the language.

Chosen. Royal. Holy. God’s own.

That is not arrogance. It is identity received from God.

And there is a reason this matters so much.

Because you cannot consistently walk toward what God has called you to become while constantly agreeing with voices that tell you who God says you are not.

You have to settle the question of identity.

Not because everyone will agree with you.

They will not.

But because your identity was never supposed to be a democratic decision.

You do not vote on what God has already spoken.

The Cross Settles Something

And if you ever wonder how much you are worth to God, look at the cross.

Do not rush past it.

Look at it.

Jesus Christ was beaten. Mocked. Rejected. Stripped. Nailed to a cross.

And He willingly gave Himself.

Romans 5:8 says:

Think about the weight of that.

He did not wait until you became perfect, or until you had your life together, or until your past was clean. Christ died for you while you were still a sinner.

The cross is not merely proof that God forgives.

It is also a revelation of how far His love was willing to go.

So when somebody tells you that you are worthless, take that accusation to Calvary.

If shame says you are disqualified, look at the cross.

Your past may insist that you can never change. Look at the empty tomb.

Rejection may tell you that nobody wants you. Remember that Christ chose to give Himself for you.

And when you are tempted to build your identity around somebody else’s opinion, remember:

The price paid for you was too great to let a human opinion become your final definition.

This does not mean you ignore correction.

God can use people to correct us.

It does not mean every criticism is false.

Sometimes people see things in us that we need to confront.

Humility listens. Repentance changes. Wisdom learns.

But correction is different from condemnation, just as a mistake is different from an identity. Conviction is not humiliation. And your past is not your future.

God can tell you, “Change,” without telling you, “You are worthless.”

That distinction matters.

The Unseen Is Not the Unreal

Hebrews 11:1 says:

Think about a seed.

Place it beneath the soil.

From above, nothing appears to be happening.

But beneath the surface, roots are forming.

Life is developing.

Something is moving that your eyes cannot yet confirm.

Then one morning, the ground breaks.

The evidence becomes visible.

The plant did not begin existing when you saw it.

You simply became aware of what had already been happening.

Visibility is not the same thing as existence.

That matters in our walk with God.

You may not see the answer yet. The provision, the opportunity and the transformation may all be hidden from you. You might not even see how God could possibly use the season you are in.

But your inability to see does not place a limitation on God’s ability to work.

Joseph could not see Egypt from the pit.

David could not see the throne from the cave.

Abraham could not see the ram from the mountain.

The disciples could not see resurrection from the tomb.

But God could.

What looks like an ending from the ground can look completely different from heaven.

Never confuse a hidden season with a finished story.

Then Jesus Said, “Come”

Matthew 14 gives us one of the clearest pictures of this.

The disciples are in a boat. The wind is against them, the waves are aggressive, and the night is dark.

Then Jesus comes walking on the water.

And Peter asks:

Jesus says:

“Come.”

Peter steps out.

Think about it.

The water did not become less dangerous because Peter stepped out.

The storm did not suddenly disappear.

Peter simply received a word from Jesus that was stronger than his fear of the water.

Then Peter looks at the wind.

He sinks.

But Jesus reaches for him.

There is another revelation here.

Peter’s mistake was not getting out of the boat.

His mistake was allowing the storm to become more real to him than the One who had called him.

How often do we do the same?

God says, “Come,” and we look at the circumstances.

When He says, “Go,” we stare at the obstacles.

He tells us, “You are Mine,” and we look at our past.

He calls us His workmanship and a new creation, and we look at somebody else’s opinion.

And the question becomes:

Whose voice will have the final word?

When God Does Not Give You the Map

Imagine being blindfolded, your hands tied. You cannot see, reach, or calculate.

Then a voice you trust says:

“Walk.”

One step.

“Keep going.”

Another.

Then your foot touches water.

You stop. Your heart races.

How deep is it? What is underneath you? Where is the other side?

So you ask:

“Where are we going?”

The voice says:

“Keep walking.”

This is what trust can feel like.

Abraham experienced something like this.

Hebrews 11:8 tells us that he went out, even though he did not know where he was going.

God gave Abraham a promise without giving him an itinerary. A destination without a map, a calling without every detail, a promise without a timetable.

Sometimes God hides the next chapter because He wants you close to the Author.

If you knew everything ahead, you might trust the plan more than the Planner.

So He gives you today’s grace for today’s step.

Tomorrow will have its own grace.

When the Water Does Not Move

Isaiah 43:2 says:

Not around them. Not necessarily before them. Through them.

God did not promise a life without waters.

He promised His presence in them.

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego entered the furnace. Daniel entered the lions’ den, and Joseph went to prison. David lived in caves. Paul suffered. Jesus entered Gethsemane.

Difficulty was never automatic evidence of God’s absence.

God sometimes delivers you from the fire. Other times He meets you in it. And you may come out of the fire looking different.

That is also deliverance.

Because perhaps the miracle is not simply that God removed the situation.

Perhaps the miracle is that the situation did not remove God from you.

You can go through something without allowing it to become you.

“I Believe; Help My Unbelief.”

Mark 9 gives us one of the most honest prayers in Scripture:

What a prayer. No performance, no pretending. Just honesty.

“I believe.
But I am struggling.”

And Jesus does not reject him for saying it.

That matters.

You can pray while afraid and worship while confused. Questions do not stop you from seeking God. Tears do not cancel trust, and exhaustion does not mean you have stopped believing.

Fear can have a voice without having authority.

God does not need your performance.

He wants your heart.

When God Says No

Perhaps the deepest surrender comes when God says no. Not when He says yes, or when the door opens, or when the prayer is answered exactly as you imagined, but when the answer is different.

Jesus showed us this in Gethsemane.

Look closely.

Jesus expressed what He wanted.

Then He surrendered it.

That is not weakness. It is trust at its deepest.

Real surrender is not:

“God, I do not care what happens.”

It is:

“God, I care deeply about what happens. But I trust You more than I trust my preferred outcome.”

That is difficult, and it is holy.

And sometimes that is where trust becomes strongest.

The Water Is Not the Destination

This is where the title of this book begins to speak.

Faith Beyond Waters.

The waters represent what is immediately in front of us: what we can see, measure, explain, and panic about.

But the water is not the whole story.

Peter saw water. Jesus saw the destination.

Where Abraham saw the absence of a lamb, God saw the ram. Joseph saw prison, and God saw Egypt. David saw a cave, while God saw a throne.

The disciples saw a tomb. God saw resurrection.

God is always seeing more than the moment you are standing in.

That is why 2 Corinthians 5:7 says:

Sight can tell you what is happening. It cannot always tell you what God is doing.

Your bank account shows what is currently there, not what God can provide. A closed door tells you that a door is closed, but not what God is doing beyond it. And a difficult season can tell you that life is hard. It cannot tell you how the story ends.

What I Have Come to Know

This is not merely theology to me.

It is something I have had to live.

There have been seasons when I had to trust God without knowing how things would unfold.

Seasons where I needed provision before I knew where it would come from, when I prayed and had to wait. Through all of it I kept writing, praying, moving and believing, even when I could not see the full picture.

And I began to notice something.

God was teaching me to stop measuring His faithfulness by how quickly He changed my circumstances.

Sometimes the answer was provision. Other times it was endurance, or redirection, or simply the strength to wake up and keep going.

Looking back, I realised that what I had called an interruption had become part of the story.

You may call it a detour while God is writing it as development.

I could not see it from where I was standing. Neither could Joseph in the pit, or David in the cave.

And perhaps that is why God asks us to trust Him.

Because He can see from places we cannot.

What Is Faith?

After all the studying, the questions, the Scriptures, the wrestling and the waiting, I keep coming back to something beautifully simple.

Faith is trust.

Trusting God when the evidence is incomplete, and His character when His timing is confusing.

Leaning on His wisdom when mine wants an explanation. Holding to His Word when circumstances are shouting something else.

Believing in His presence when I cannot feel it, and in His goodness when I cannot yet see the good.

Trusting Him whether the answer is yes or no, whether the water parts or remains.

And trusting Him when He tells me who I am.

Because perhaps one of the most overlooked expressions of trust is this:

Believing God about yourself.

Not your feelings, your critics, your past, comparison or shame.

God.

If He says you are His, believe Him. Believe Him when He calls you His workmanship and a new creation, when He says there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus, and when He calls you chosen, beloved, redeemed, and being transformed.

You may still be growing, with things to change and wounds to heal. There may still be areas where God is correcting you.

But correction does not cancel identity.

God can correct your behavior without rejecting your worth.

And your past may be part of your testimony without becoming the title of your life.

Beyond the Waters

Maybe you are reading this from a boat right now, and the wind is against you. Something you prayed about has not changed. You are standing at the edge of something God is asking you to step into, or you have been staring at the water so long that you have forgotten to look at Jesus.

Look again.

The water is not bigger than Him, and the storm is not stronger. Nothing unknown is unknown to Him. The future is not waiting for Him to discover it.

He is already there.

And you do not need tomorrow’s strength today.

You need today’s grace, today’s Word, today’s obedience.

One step. Then another, and another.

Until one day you look back and realize something:

The goal was never simply to get you across the water. It was to teach you that you could trust Him in it.

Because one day, the waters will be behind you.

And when you look back, you may realize the greatest miracle was not that the water disappeared.

It was that you discovered who God was while you were standing in it.

And perhaps that is what it means to go beyond the waters.

Not to live without uncertainty or storms, or to have every answer.

But to reach a place where the presence of God becomes more certain to you than the circumstances around you.

Where you can say:

“I do not know what tomorrow holds.
But I know who holds tomorrow.”

And mean it.

Not because everything worked out exactly as you planned.

But because somewhere along the journey, you discovered something deeper than an answer.

You discovered God.

And once you know Him, the question changes.

You stop asking only:

“What will God do?”

And you begin asking:

“Who is God to me when I cannot see what He is doing?”

That question will take us deeper.

Because the waters are only the beginning.

Limiting Your Mind

18 min read12 readings30 passages of Scripture

The idea for this book sat on my heart for a long time before I wrote a single word of it.

I would be praying, or driving, or sitting in church, and a line would come to me. Then another. I typed them into the notes on my phone and told myself, one day.

One day kept moving.

Because every time I thought about actually writing it, a quiet voice would ask me a question.

“Who are you to write a book?”

You are not a pastor. You did not go to seminary. You work in sales. Real authors are other people. People with platforms. People who have it all figured out.

Nobody had ever said those words to me out loud. But I had heard versions of them for so long, from the world and from my own head, that I had started treating them like facts.

So the notes kept piling up, and the book stayed on my phone.

Then one day, reading Scripture, something hit me. God has never once checked a résumé before He used someone. He used shepherds and fishermen, farmers and a tax collector. Not one of them was waiting until they felt qualified.

So I opened a blank page and wrote the first line.

The book you are reading right now exists because I finally stopped agreeing with a sentence.

That season taught me something I have never forgotten.

Most limits are not made of walls.

They are made of sentences.

Sentences you heard once and never stopped hearing.

“You are not that kind of person.”

“People like us do not do things like that.”

“Be realistic.”

“Know your place.”

Nobody built a fence around you. Somebody just handed you a sentence, and you built the fence yourself.

You built it quietly. Over years. One small agreement at a time.

And now you live inside it.

You call it being sensible, or humble, or knowing your limits.

But some of what you call your limits are not your limits at all.

They are your borders.

And borders can be crossed.

A wall stops you from the outside.

A belief stops you from the inside, and calls it wisdom.

This chapter is about the most dangerous ceiling a person can live under.

Not the one in the room.

The one in the mind.

Because before God takes you anywhere, He usually has to take something out of you.

A small picture, a small expectation, a small view of God.

The Most Expensive Sentence in the Bible

Let me take you somewhere.

Israel is standing at the edge of the land God promised them. Everything God said is now within walking distance. Moses sends twelve men to look at the land before they go in.

They come back with fruit so large that it takes two men to carry a single cluster of grapes on a pole.

The land is everything God said it would be.

And then ten of the twelve men open their mouths.

Read the last line again.

“We seemed like grasshoppers in our own eyes, and we looked the same to them.”

Here is something I want you to notice.

The giants never said it.

Nowhere in the story does a single giant look at Israel and call them grasshoppers. There is no conversation. No insult. No recorded opinion.

The spies decided how they looked to themselves, and then they assumed everyone else saw them the same way.

That is how a limited mind works.

It does not wait for the world to reject you. It rejects you in advance, on the world’s behalf.

You do not apply because you have already decided what they will say. I know, because I almost never wrote this book.

You stay quiet because you have decided no one will listen. You never start, because you have already decided how it ends.

You become the giant in your own story, and the grasshopper too.

And that one sentence cost a whole generation everything.

Here is a detail that is easy to miss.

Eleven days.

The journey to the edge of the promise was eleven days long.

It took them forty years.

Not because the road was long.

Because the mindset was.

Distance was never the problem.

Definition was.

Some of us are not far from what God promised.

We are just still walking in circles around a sentence.

God Took Him Outside

Now look at Abram.

God had promised him a son. Years passed. Nothing happened. Abram is old. Sarah is old. The promise is getting harder to believe with every birthday.

And one night God does something beautiful.

“He took him outside.”

God took Abram outside.

Why?

Because you cannot see the stars from inside a tent.

Abram had been looking at the promise from under a low roof. Inside the tent, all he could see were the same walls, the same years, the same disappointment, and from there the promise looked impossible.

So God did not just repeat the promise. He changed Abram’s view.

Some promises cannot be received under a low ceiling.

So God takes you outside.

There are seasons when God does not change your situation first.

He changes your sky.

He moves you out of the small room you have been thinking in, and lets you look up at something too big to count.

And here is what makes it even more striking.

Earlier, God had said this to Abram:

“All the land that you see.”

God connected what Abram would receive to what Abram could see.

God tied the inheritance to the eyesight.

I am not saying you can imagine your way into anything you want. Faith is not a wish list.

But I am saying this.

A person who cannot see past their current address will struggle to walk into the place God is calling them to.

You will not pursue what you cannot picture.

God Does Not Call You by Your Current Address

Look at Gideon.

When we meet him, he is not on a battlefield. He is hiding.

Threshing wheat is supposed to happen in an open place, where the wind can carry the chaff away. Gideon is doing it in a pit, out of sight, because he is afraid.

And that is exactly where the angel of the Lord finds him.

Mighty warrior.

To a man hiding in a hole.

Gideon’s answer tells you everything about how he saw himself.

Weakest clan.

Least in my family.

He gave God his background, his ranking, and his résumé of reasons.

God gave him a new name.

God does not call you by your current address.

He calls you by your destination.

Moses did the same thing.

When God called him at the burning bush, Moses asked a question that many of us are still asking:

And look closely at how God answered.

God never answered the question.

Moses asked, “Who am I?”

God said, “I will be with you.”

That is a revelation worth sitting with.

Your sense of inadequacy is not always solved by discovering how capable you are. Sometimes it is solved by discovering how present God is.

The question “Who am I?” becomes a lot less heavy when it is answered by “I AM.”

Jeremiah tried it too.

And God would not let him finish the sentence.

Do not say it. Not even once.

God is not only interested in what you do. He is interested in what you say about yourself, because what you keep saying, you eventually start obeying.

Your Job Title Is Not Your Destiny

Let me talk about work for a moment, because this is where many of us live every day.

We introduce ourselves by our job titles.

“I am a cashier.” “I am a nurse.” “I am just an assistant.” “I am a student.” “I am in between jobs.”

And slowly, without noticing, the title stops being a description of what we do and becomes a definition of who we are.

Then it becomes a ceiling.

Look at David.

When the prophet Samuel came to Jesse’s house to anoint the next king of Israel, Jesse lined up his sons. Seven of them. Not one was chosen.

Samuel asked if there were any others.

Notice that.

They did not even invite him.

In his own father’s mind, David’s occupation had already disqualified him from the room. He was the shepherd. The youngest. The one with the smell of the field on him.

But God was not looking at the field.

And here is the deeper revelation.

The field was not wasted.

The same hands that fought a lion and a bear to protect sheep would one day face Goliath. The patience that led a flock would lead a nation, and the songs he sang alone on the hills would fill the book of Psalms.

The field was not David’s limit.

It was David’s classroom.

Your current job may be the place God is training the exact muscle your future will need.

Amos understood this.

A shepherd. A fig farmer.

Not from a prophet’s family. No pedigree. No title.

And God took him while he was tending the flock.

God did not wait for Amos to change careers before He called him. He called him in the middle of an ordinary workday.

Peter was a fisherman. Jesus did not tell him to stop being who he was. He said:

Jesus did not erase the occupation.

He enlarged it.

Daniel was taken from his home as a young man and carried off to Babylon. A foreigner. A captive. Placed in a government that did not share his faith. Many would have assumed his career was over before it started.

Instead, he served under king after king, and rose so high that the king planned to set him over the whole kingdom. He never stopped praying three times a day, and he never let a foreign job title tell him who he was.

The kings changed.

The God on the job did not.

And now look at the most striking example of all.

When Jesus returned to His hometown and began teaching, the people were amazed. And then they said this:

They tried to limit the Son of God with a job title.

And listen to what Scripture says happened next.

That should stop all of us.

The same Jesus who calmed storms and raised the dead did very few miracles in Nazareth. Not because His power had changed. Because their perspective had.

They had known Him too long to see Him clearly.

Familiarity put a ceiling on what faith could have opened.

If people boxed in Jesus by His occupation, do not be surprised when people try to box in you.

But do not help them.

Your job title describes what you do this season.

It was never designed to describe what God can do through you.

The Classroom You Came From

Some of us are limited by a job title.

Others are limited by a certificate. Or the lack of one.

“I did not finish school.” “I did not go to the right university.” “I am not smart enough for that.” “I am too old to start learning now.”

Education matters. Learning is a gift. Growth honours God.

But listen to this.

After Peter and John healed a man and preached with boldness, the religious leaders of their day, highly educated men, looked at them and did not know what to do.

Unschooled.

Ordinary.

And yet the experts were astonished.

What explained it?

“These men had been with Jesus.”

Their credential was proximity.

That does not mean you should stop learning. Please keep learning. Read. Study. Enroll. Grow.

But never let a missing certificate convince you that God has a missing plan.

Your background may explain your starting point.

It does not get to predict your finishing line.

Limiting the Holy One

Now we come to the deepest part of this chapter.

Because the most dangerous thing about a limited mind is not what it does to how you see yourself.

It is what it does to how you see God.

There is a verse in the Psalms that I think should make every believer pause.

Limited the Holy One of Israel.

Think about that.

The God who split the sea, rained bread from heaven, and brought water out of a rock.

And His own people limited Him.

How?

The very next verse tells us.

They forgot.

That is how they limited Him.

Not with their hands. With their memory.

Israel did not limit God with their hands.

They limited Him with their memory.

Every time you forget what God has already done, the God in your mind gets a little smaller.

And a small view of God cannot carry a big future.

This is why remembering is a spiritual discipline. Write down what God has done and speak it. Tell your children, and tell yourself too, on the days when the current problem looks bigger than your history with God.

Because your problem is not the only thing that is real.

Your testimony is real too.

Then there is this:

Far more than you could ever imagine, beyond your wildest dreams.

Do you see what Paul is saying?

The highest thing you can imagine is not God’s ceiling.

Your highest thought is His starting line.

And God Himself said:

So when you say, “I cannot see how this could work,” you are telling the truth.

You cannot.

But that has never been the requirement.

The Oil Stopped When the Jars Ran Out

There is a story in 2 Kings that I believe captures this whole chapter.

A widow is in debt. Her husband has died. The creditors are coming to take her two sons as slaves. All she has left in her house is one small pot of oil.

The prophet Elisha gives her a strange instruction.

Don’t ask for just a few.

Then she was to go inside, shut the door, and start pouring.

And the oil kept flowing. Jar after jar after jar.

Until this moment:

The oil stopped flowing.

Not because God ran out.

Because the jars did.

God filled every vessel she prepared.

The only limit on the miracle was the size of her preparation.

How many jars did you borrow?

How much room have you made for what God wants to pour?

Some of us are praying for overflow while carrying one cup.

There is another story, just a few chapters later, that hits even harder.

King Joash goes to the dying prophet Elisha. Elisha tells him to take arrows and strike the ground.

He struck three times and stopped.

Not because his arm was tired.

Because his expectation was.

Three strikes meant three victories. Not total victory. Three.

The king set the size of his own breakthrough by how long he was willing to keep striking.

Do not strike three times at a promise God meant for six.

Open His Eyes

There is one more story I want you to see.

The king of Syria sends an army by night to capture Elisha. In the morning, Elisha’s servant walks outside and sees the city surrounded by horses and chariots.

He panics.

“Oh no, my lord! What shall we do?”

Elisha is calm. He tells his servant not to fear, because those who are with them are more than those who are against them.

Then Elisha prays a prayer I want to pray over you.

Here is the revelation.

Nothing on the hills changed.

The chariots of fire were already there.

The only thing that changed was the servant’s eyes.

He did not need a bigger army.

He needed bigger eyes.

Sometimes we pray for God to send help, when what we really need is for God to show us the help He already sent.

You are not as alone as you think, or as behind, or as small.

What I Had to Unlearn

Let me be honest with you.

This chapter is not theory to me.

My roots are in Imo State, Nigeria. Today I live and write from Ottawa, Canada. Somewhere between those two places, I had to learn that geography does not get to decide destiny.

Years ago, I stepped into the world of sales. I started at the very beginning, in a role whose whole job is to hear no and keep going. From there I led a team, became an account executive, and later a senior account executive.

And here is what nobody writes on a résumé.

Every level asked me to bury a smaller version of myself first.

The thinking that got me into one room could not carry me into the next. The title only changed after the thinking did.

And now, as you already know, I am writing a book. God is not limited by the words we reach for first. He keeps adding chapters to people who thought their story was already written.

So when I tell you not to limit your mind, I am not speaking from a stage.

I am speaking from the road.

Enlarge Your Tent

So what do we do?

How do we stop living inside a sentence someone else wrote?

God gave Israel an instruction through Isaiah that I believe is still for us today.

Enlarge.

Stretch.

Do not hold back.

Lengthen.

Strengthen.

Notice that God did not just say, “Make your tent bigger.” He said, “Strengthen your stakes.”

Because when you stretch the tent, the wind has more to push against.

A bigger vision needs deeper roots, or the first storm will fold it.

Growth will be tested. Expansion will cost you. Some people will not understand the new size of your tent. Do not let that make you shrink it again. Strengthen your stakes instead.

And the stakes are planted in one place: the mind.

Transformation is not only something God does to your circumstances.

It is something He does to your thinking.

So here is how we begin.

  • Catch the sentence. Notice the words you say about yourself when nobody is listening. Write them down. Ask honestly, “Did God say this, or did someone else?”
  • Replace the sentence. Find what God has said instead. Read it out loud. Let His word be the loudest voice in the room.
  • Remember the hand. Keep a record of what God has already done. A God you remember is a God you will not limit.
  • Borrow more jars. Prepare for what you are praying for. Apply. Enroll. Write. Start. Make room before the oil arrives.
  • Strike again. Do not stop at three when God is talking about six. Keep going after the point where most people get comfortable.
  • Go outside. Get out of the small tent you have been thinking in. Look up. Count what you can. Then let God tell you, “So shall it be.”

Beyond the Ceiling

I want to leave you with this.

No eye has seen it, no ear has heard it, and no human mind has even conceived it.

That means the best you have ever imagined for your life is still smaller than what God has prepared.

Your career is not the ceiling. Neither is your education, your background, your past, or your current season.

And the opinion people formed of you years ago is definitely not the ceiling.

Somewhere above all of it, God is still saying what He said to Abram.

Come outside.

Look up.

Count if you can.

You were never too small for the plan, just looking at it from inside the tent.

So step outside.

Let God stretch your thinking until it matches His promise.

Let Him take the sentence you have been living inside of and replace it with His word.

And when the old voice whispers, “Be realistic,” answer it with the most realistic thing in the universe:

God is able.

Far more than you could ever imagine.

Far beyond your wildest dreams.

The tent was never the limit.

The sky was always the plan.

Praise

17 min read11 readings24 passages of Scripture

Somewhere along this journey, I realised something that changed the way I pray.

I used to think praise was what you did after God came through.

The answer arrives. The door opens. The money lands. The test result comes back clear. Then you lift your hands and say thank You.

Praise was the receipt.

But the deeper I went into Scripture, the more I saw that the people who walked closest to God did not treat praise as a receipt.

They treated it as a response.

Not a response to what God had done.

A response to who God is.

And that changes everything. Because what God does can look different from season to season. But who God is never changes.

So if praise is a response to who He is, then there is never a season where praise is out of place.

Faith believes God.

Praise says it out loud.

That is what this chapter is about.

Not praise as a song we sing at the start of a church service.

Praise as a way of living, as the sound faith makes, as the thing that keeps your heart pointed at God when everything else is trying to turn it away.

David put it like this:

At all times.

Beautiful words. Easy to put on a mug or a wall.

But do you know when David wrote them?

The heading of Psalm 34 tells us. David wrote this psalm after a moment when he was running for his life. He had fled into enemy territory, and when he was recognised, he was so afraid that he pretended to be insane. Scripture says he scratched on the doors of the gate and let spit run down his beard, so the king would think he was mad and send him away.

That was the background of “at all times.”

Not a palace or a victory, but one of the most humiliating days of his life.

David did not write “at all times” on his best day.

He wrote it on one of his worst.

So when Scripture tells us to praise at all times, it is not speaking from a comfortable distance. It is speaking from the floor. From the place where you have nothing left but your breath and your God.

And that is exactly where my own lesson began.

The Email

There was a season when I was looking for a job.

And I mean really looking. I was doing everything. Applications. Preparation. Research. Follow-ups. Every door I could knock on, I knocked on.

Then one opportunity started to move.

I passed stage one, then stage two, then stage three.

I remember that company so well. I thought I had done well, and I let myself picture it. The role. The start date. The relief.

And then the email came.

You probably know the kind.

“Unfortunately, we have decided to move forward with other applicants.”

I read it, and something in me dropped.

I felt bad, then sad. And if I am being completely honest with you, at some point I felt angry. I had done everything I knew to do: prayed, prepared, shown up. It still did not happen.

Then my mom said something to me that I have never forgotten.

“Do you know that you are supposed to be giving God thanks?”

And then she said the line that went straight through me.

That hit me so hard.

Because she was right.

My first reaction had been disappointment. My second reaction was frustration. Praise was not even on the list.

I had been praising God for the doors I hoped would open, but I had not learned to praise Him at the doors that closed.

I did not get that job.

But I got something I needed more.

A question I now ask myself in every hard moment.

What is my first reaction going to be?

Not long after that, I got the chance to test it.

I opened my laptop one morning to an email from a different company. Another “unfortunately.” And I felt that same old sinking feeling start to rise in my chest.

But this time, before I let myself spiral, I closed the laptop. I took a breath. And I said out loud, alone in my apartment, “Lord, thank You. You are still good, and You still know where I am going.”

I would love to tell you the disappointment disappeared instantly.

It did not.

But something was different. The disappointment was still in the car.

It just was not driving anymore.

Praise was.

Judah Goes First

It turns out my mom was preaching something that runs through the whole Bible.

Let me show you.

In Genesis, there is a woman named Leah. She is married to Jacob, but Jacob loves her sister Rachel. Leah knows it. Everybody knows it.

So Leah has children, and with every child, you can hear her heart.

Her first son, she names Reuben, and says:

Her second son, she names Simeon, because the Lord heard that she was not loved.

Her third son, Levi, because now, surely, her husband will be joined to her.

Three children.

Three names that all say the same thing.

“Maybe now he will love me.”

Then comes her fourth son.

This time I will praise the Lord.

Those two words, this time, mark a turning point. Leah’s situation had not changed. Jacob had not suddenly fallen in love with her. But she stopped naming her children after her pain, and she named one after her praise.

Judah means praise.

And here is the part that I want you to see.

Out of all of Jacob’s sons, it was through Judah that the line of kings would come. King David came from Judah. And Jesus Christ Himself is called “the Lion of the tribe of Juda.”

Leah did not know what her praise would carry.

Neither do you.

But it gets even better.

After the death of Joshua, Israel asked God a question:

Who goes first?

Judah.

Praise.

My mom did not know she was quoting the book of Judges. But she was.

Ahead of the strategy, the explanation, the next application, even the answer.

Praise goes first.

And then there is King Jehoshaphat.

Three armies were marching against him. He was outnumbered and afraid. So he prayed, and God told him the battle was not his but God’s. Then Jehoshaphat did something no military leader would ever do.

He put the choir in front of the soldiers.

And look at the timing.

“As they began to sing.”

Not when they finished. Not after the third song. Not once they felt better.

As they began.

Praise is not the celebration after the battle.

Sometimes praise is the battle plan.

Look Who You Are Praising

Let me tell you why praise makes sense even when life does not.

Because of who we are praising.

Sometimes we forget who God really is. We shrink Him down to the size of our problem. We talk about Him as if He is just slightly bigger than our bills, or just slightly stronger than our fear.

But think about it.

Our Saviour walks on water.

The same water that terrified the disciples was a floor under His feet. The storm that made grown fishermen scream was something He could speak to and silence.

Your problem is not just smaller than Him. It is under His feet.

Scripture says:

A name above every name.

Your diagnosis, the company that said no, the debt, the person who walked away: His name is above all of them.

Every name you are afraid of has to bow to His.

And Jesus Himself said:

All.

Not most. Not some. All.

That means God can change your situation in one night.

In the book of Esther, a king could not sleep one night, asked for the records to be read to him, and discovered that a man named Mordecai had once saved his life and never been rewarded. By the next day, Mordecai was being led through the city in royal robes. One night can undo years.

But here is what moves me even more than His power.

I once heard a pastor say something that I have never been able to shake. He said, think about who we are talking about. The Almighty God. The One who holds everything together. And yet, in our hardest moments, He still makes time to come. He comes down to our level. He sits with us in it, just to comfort us.

That is our God.

Scripture says it too:

Read that slowly.

The high and exalted One, who lives forever, has two addresses.

One is the high and holy place.

The other is next to a broken heart.

God has two addresses.

Heaven, and wherever someone is hurting.

That verse is in the same psalm as “I will bless the LORD at all times.” Of course it is. David could praise at his lowest because he had discovered that his lowest was exactly where God came closest.

Jesus is not a Saviour who watches from a distance.

He is able to empathize.

He feels it with you.

At the tomb of His friend Lazarus, knowing full well that He was about to raise him from the dead, Jesus still stopped and wept.

Power that could raise the dead.

Tenderness that would not rush your tears.

That is who we are praising.

Praise Is Faith Out Loud

Now let me show you how deeply praise and faith are connected.

When Scripture describes Abraham waiting for the son God had promised, it says this:

Strengthened in his faith, and gave glory to God.

Those two things are side by side for a reason.

Abraham did not give glory to God after Isaac was born. He gave glory while he was still waiting. While his body was old. While Sarah was still barren. While nothing in the natural had changed.

And the giving of glory was part of what kept his faith strong.

Here is the revelation.

Praise is how faith says thank You before the answer arrives.

When you praise God in the waiting, you are making a declaration. You are saying, “I do not have to see it to know who You are.”

Worry rehearses the problem.

Praise rehearses the promise.

And whichever one you rehearse the most is the one that grows.

The prophet Habakkuk understood this.

Look at the list.

No figs. No grapes. No olives. No harvest. No sheep. No cattle.

In his world, that was the complete collapse of the economy. No income. No savings. No backup plan.

And then one word changes the whole passage.

Yet.

Yet is the most powerful word a believer can say.

Yet means the situation has spoken, but it does not get the last word. I can see the empty field and still choose joy, because my praise is not attached to my circumstances. It is attached to my God.

Midnight in Philippi

Paul and Silas had been beaten with rods, thrown into the inner prison, and had their feet locked in stocks.

Their backs were bleeding. Their legs were chained. It was the middle of the night.

And this is what they did:

Notice two things.

First, “the other prisoners were listening to them.”

Your praise in a hard season is never private. Someone is listening. Someone in the cell next to you, chained by their own situation, is hearing you sing and wondering what kind of God is worth singing to in a place like this.

Your praise might be the only sermon they ever hear.

Second, “everyone’s chains came loose.”

Not just Paul’s, or Silas’s.

Everyone’s.

When you choose to praise in the middle of your midnight, you never know whose freedom is attached to your song.

He Lives in It

There is a verse that I believe explains why praise is so powerful.

God inhabits praise.

He makes His home in it.

When we praise, we are not just sending words up. We are making room for God to come in.

But here is what most people miss.

Do you know how Psalm 22 begins?

“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

These are the same words Jesus cried from the cross.

The verse about God living in our praise sits inside the psalm of feeling abandoned.

David felt forsaken in verse one, and by verse three he was declaring that God is holy and lives in the praises of His people.

He did not wait to feel better.

He praised his way back to the truth.

Praise does not deny the pain.

It just refuses to let pain have the microphone.

And then there is Job.

In a single day, Job lost his wealth, his servants, and all ten of his children. Messenger after messenger came with worse news than the last.

And Scripture records his first reaction.

Look at the order.

He tore his robe. He grieved. He was human. He felt it.

And then he fell to the ground in worship.

Job’s grief was real. But it was not his final word. His first reaction was to bring his pain to the feet of God.

That is what my mom was talking about.

Praise does not mean you never feel the loss.

It means that when the loss comes, you know which direction to fall.

What Praise Does to You

Praise changes things around us.

But it also changes things in us.

Isaiah describes it like clothing:

A garment.

You do not wait to feel dressed before you put on clothes. You put them on, and then you are dressed.

Praise works the same way. You do not always wait until you feel like praising. Sometimes you put it on, and the heaviness begins to lift.

Scripture also says:

Long before anyone studied it, the Bible was saying that what happens in your heart affects your whole being.

And today, many researchers who study gratitude have found it linked to things like lower stress, better sleep, and a more hopeful outlook. That should not surprise us. God designed us. Of course the posture He invites us into is good for us.

Think about it practically.

It is almost impossible to panic and praise at full volume at the same time. One of them has to give way.

When you praise, you lift your eyes from the problem to the One who is bigger than it. Your mind stops circling the worst case. Your breathing slows. Your heart remembers.

David did this on purpose.

Notice what David is doing.

He is talking to himself.

Instead of listening to his soul, he is speaking to it.

Stop listening to your soul and start preaching to it.

Your feelings will always have an opinion.

Praise reminds them who is in charge.

Praise on the Mountaintop

I do not want you to think praise is only for the hard days.

This book is not about surviving life. It is about walking with God through all of it. And that includes the good days.

The promotions. The acceptance letters. The healing. The wedding. The new baby. The breakthrough you prayed about for years.

Here is something interesting. Scripture actually warns us about the good seasons.

Be careful when you are satisfied.

Because the danger of the good season is not that we curse God.

It is that we forget Him.

Hard times push us toward God. Good times can quietly drift us away from Him.

Praise on the mountaintop keeps you humble. It reminds you that the view is a gift and not an achievement.

Remember the ten lepers?

Jesus healed all ten. Every one of them was cleansed and walked away with their skin made new.

But only one came back.

He came back shouting praise and fell at Jesus’ feet giving thanks. And Jesus said something to him that He did not say to the other nine:

Nine were cleansed.

One was made whole.

The difference was not the miracle. It was the response.

Praise on your good days does not just say thank You. It invites God deeper into the blessing, until the blessing becomes something more than a gift. It becomes a relationship.

In Everything

So how do we live this out?

Paul gives us a simple instruction:

Read it carefully.

It does not say for all circumstances.

It says in all circumstances.

You do not have to thank God for the rejection email, or pretend the diagnosis is good news, or act like the betrayal did not hurt.

But in all circumstances, in the middle of it, you can still give thanks. God is still God. He is still good, and He is still with you.

And sometimes, that kind of praise will cost you something.

A sacrifice of praise. In other words, praise that costs you something.

Praising God with tears still on your face is a sacrifice. So is thanking Him before you understand, or saying “You are good” while your feelings say no.

And heaven treasures it.

So here is what I am learning to do. And I invite you to do it with me.

  • Watch your first reaction. When the news comes, good or bad, let your first words be toward God. Even if it is just, “You are still good.”
  • Name one thing. In the hardest moment, find one thing to thank Him for. Breath. A friend. Yesterday’s grace. Start small. Praise grows.
  • Speak to your soul. Do not just feel your way through the day. Tell your soul what God has said.
  • Remember out loud. Say what God has already done. Your history with Him is evidence for your future with Him.
  • Go back like the one leper. When the blessing comes, return to the One who gave it before you run off to enjoy it.
  • Sing at midnight. Do not wait for morning to praise. Someone in the next cell is listening.

Let Everything That Has Breath

The very last verse of the book of Psalms says this:

Here is the qualification for praise.

Not a job, a breakthrough, or a perfect week.

Breath.

Let your first reaction be praise.

Not because everything is good, but because He is.

You may not see the way, yet you know the One who walks on water.

The night may not be over. Joy is still coming in the morning.

When praise goes first, faith is never far behind.

My mom was right.

God is looking at your first reaction.

Let Him find you praising.

Where Are You?

16 min read11 readings28 passages of Scripture

The first question God ever asked a human being was not, “What did you do?”

Nor was it, “Why did you disobey Me?” or “How could you?”

It was three words.

Think about that.

God knows everything. He knew exactly where Adam was standing, which tree he was hiding behind, what had happened and why.

So why ask?

Because God was not asking for information.

He was asking for Adam.

That question tells you more about the heart of God than almost anything else in the Bible.

The first thing God did after humanity broke His heart was not to walk away.

It was to come looking.

And He is still asking that question today. Not in a garden this time, but in your car on the way to work. In your quiet bedroom at night. In the middle of your busy week, when your calendar is full and your heart is somewhere far away.

Where are you?

This chapter is about the God who keeps asking.

Before There Was a Law, There Was a Walk

Long before the Ten Commandments, the temple, the sacrifices, the priests, the rules.

There was a walk.

The LORD God was walking in the garden in the cool of the day.

That one line paints a picture of what life with God was meant to be. Not a meeting or a ritual, but a walk. An evening stroll. Two companions in the cool of the day.

Adam and Eve recognised the sound of His footsteps. That means they had heard them before.

This was not the first walk.

It was a habit.

Before there was a law, there was a walk.

And if you go back even further, you find something remarkable about who God is.

Us. Our.

Before there was a single human being, God was already in relationship: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

That does not mean three separate gods, or one God with two helpers beside Him. Scripture is clear that there is only one God. “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). The Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are each fully God, yet never apart, never divided, and never competing. One God, always together.

That is why the Bible can say “God is love” (1 John 4:8). Love needs someone to love, and before anything else existed, the one God was already Father, Son and Spirit, loving and being loved. Love did not begin with us. Within God there has always been perfect love, perfect fellowship, perfect unity.

God did not create you because He was lonely.

He created you because He is love, and love makes room for more.

You were not designed to simply believe in God from a distance, but to walk with Him.

The God Who Wants to Be Known

There is something about God that many people never realise.

He does not just want to be obeyed.

He wants to be known.

Jesus said something that changes how we understand the whole Christian life:

Read how Jesus defines eternal life.

Not just living forever, or going to heaven when you die.

Knowing God.

Eternal life is not only a destination. It is a relationship that starts now.

And then Jesus said this to His disciples:

Friends.

The Creator of the universe uses the word friends.

A servant knows what to do, while a friend knows who you are. Servants wait for instructions. Friends are invited into your heart.

God is not looking for employees.

He is looking for friends.

Moses knew this kind of friendship.

Face to face.

As one speaks to a friend.

That is what God wants with you.

Not a God you visit once a week and forget the rest of the time, or only call when you need something. A God you talk to the way you talk to the people you love most.

And He is not waiting for you to find the courage to go looking for Him.

He is already at the door.

Notice what He wants to do when He comes in.

Eat with you.

In the culture of the Bible, sharing a meal was one of the most intimate things you could do. It meant friendship. Acceptance. Belonging.

Jesus is not knocking to inspect your house.

He is knocking to sit at your table.

When Life Gets Good

Now let me share something I had to learn for myself.

Life can be hard. We all know that. But life can also be good. Really good. And at some point, I came to understand something about the good seasons that I had never noticed before.

When life is good, we tend to forget the One who makes it good.

Not on purpose.

Nobody wakes up and says, “Today I will forget God.”

It happens quietly.

The prayers get shorter. The Bible stays closed a little longer. The gratitude fades into routine. We are busy, we are blessed, things are working, and slowly, without even noticing, God moves from the centre of our lives to the edges.

We go about our life, and we forget Him.

I know, because I have done it.

I remember a season when everything seemed to be working. Work was going well. The bills were paid on time. My weekends were full of friends, plans and good food. If you had asked me, I would have told you I was blessed, and I would have meant it.

Then one Sunday at Lighthouse Church, in the middle of worship, the music dropped and the room went quiet. And in that quiet, a simple question rose up in my heart.

When was the last time you talked to Me, and not just about what you need?

I could not remember.

I had been thanking God in passing. A quick “thank You, Lord” before a meal. A short prayer on the way to a meeting. But I had not really sat with Him in weeks. I had been enjoying the blessing and slowly drifting from the One who blessed me.

Nothing was wrong in my life.

That was exactly the problem.

With nothing wrong, nothing was pulling me back to Him.

That afternoon, I went home, put my phone face down on the table, opened my Bible, and just talked to Him. No list and no requests. Just the way you talk to a friend you have been neglecting and have really missed.

It felt like coming home.

God saw this happen to His people again and again.

Look at the progression.

Fed.

Satisfied.

Proud.

Forgot.

That is the slow drift of the human heart. It does not happen in one dramatic moment. It happens one comfortable step at a time.

Hard times can push you away from God.

Good times can drift you away from Him.

And here is what makes this so important.

The danger of the drift is not that God stops loving you.

It is that you stop knowing Him.

You can be surrounded by everything God has given you and still be far from God Himself.

When God Says Not Yet

Which brings me to something that took me a long time to understand.

I have realised that sometimes God removes certain things from our lives, or does not allow certain things to happen, and it is not because He does not want us to have them.

He is not holding out on us, and He is not being unkind.

Sometimes it is because He knows our hearts better than we do.

He knows that if He gives us that thing right now, we will forget about Him. Or that we will start to worship the gift instead of the Giver. That the thing we are praying for could quietly take His place.

And God loves us too much to let anything take His place.

There is a verse in the Psalms that shook me when I first understood it. It is about Israel in the wilderness, demanding what they wanted.

He gave them what they asked for.

And their souls became thin.

Full hands. Empty heart.

Sometimes the most dangerous answer to prayer is getting what you want without getting more of God.

Israel did it again years later. They looked at the nations around them and demanded a king.

And God told Samuel the truth about what was really happening.

They thought they were asking for a king.

They were really asking to replace God.

So when God says no, or not yet, it is worth asking a different question. Not, “Why won’t God give me this?” but, “What would this become if He did?”

Look at Abraham.

God had promised him a son. Abraham waited twenty five years, and then Isaac was born, the long awaited miracle, the son of the promise.

Then God asked for him back.

It is one of the hardest passages in the whole Bible.

But look at how it ends.

Abraham obeyed, all the way up the mountain, all the way to the altar. And at the last moment, God stopped him.

God never wanted to take Isaac.

He wanted to make sure Isaac had not taken Abraham.

Abraham walked down that mountain with his son beside him. He did not lose the gift. He simply settled forever that the Giver came first.

Then there is the rich young man who came running to Jesus.

Do not miss the first line.

“Jesus looked at him and loved him.”

Before Jesus asked him to let go of anything, He loved him. The request did not come from a God who wanted to take. It came from a God who wanted him.

But the man’s possessions had become his god. And he walked away sad.

When God asks you to loosen your grip, it is never because He wants less for you.

It is because He wants you.

Moses Said No to the Promised Land

This next story is one that I believe very few people have truly noticed.

After Israel made the golden calf, God told Moses something shocking. He said He would still give them the land He had promised. The land flowing with milk and honey. Everything they had been dreaming of.

But there was one condition.

Read that again.

You can have the promise.

You just will not have Me.

Many people would have taken that deal. The land was the dream, the whole point of leaving Egypt.

But Moses said no.

Moses was offered the Promised Land without the One who promised it.

And he refused.

That is what a real relationship with God looks like.

The gift is good.

But it is not the goal.

The goal is Him.

And God’s answer to Moses is beautiful.

I know you by name.

That is not the language of a ruler to a subject.

It is the language of a friend.

When Moses chose God over the gift, he ended up with both.

Busy for Him, or With Him?

One of the easiest ways to lose a relationship with God is to replace it with activity for God.

Jesus visited the home of two sisters, Martha and Mary.

Martha was not doing anything wrong. She was serving, hosting, working hard for Jesus.

But she was so busy for Him that she missed being with Him.

Only one thing is needed.

Not more serving.

More sitting.

Martha was busy for Jesus.

Mary was with Him.

You can be in church every Sunday, serve on every team, and still be distant from God. It is possible to know a lot about Him and not really know Him.

Jesus said Mary chose the better thing, and that it would never be taken from her.

Your achievements can be taken. Your position can be taken. Your possessions can be taken.

But time spent at His feet becomes something no one can ever take away.

Two Sons, One Father

You probably know the story of the prodigal son.

The younger son demanded his inheritance, left home, wasted everything, and came back broken and ashamed.

And while he was still rehearsing his apology, this happened:

The father saw him while he was still a long way off.

Which means the father had been watching the road.

Every day.

That is the heart of God.

But there is a second son in this story, and I think he might be the most important one.

The older brother never left. He stayed home. He worked hard. He followed every rule. And when the celebration started for his younger brother, he refused to go in.

Slaving.

That word tells you everything. He lived in his father’s house like an employee, not a son.

And listen to the father’s answer.

Everything I have is yours.

He had access to everything the whole time. He just never enjoyed it, because he never knew his father’s heart.

One son left the house.

The other never left, and still never came home.

You can be near God and still miss His heart.

The father in this story wanted the same thing from both sons.

Not their work.

Them.

Faith Grows Where Relationship Lives

Now let me connect all of this to the heart of this book.

Faith.

Here is how the Bible describes the kind of faith that pleases God:

Look at the words in that verse.

Comes to him.

Seek him.

Faith in the Bible is not just believing a set of facts about God. It is coming to Him. Seeking Him. Moving toward Him.

Faith is relational.

That is why the closer you walk with God, the easier it becomes to stand on His word when life gets difficult. You are not trusting a stranger, but Someone you have walked with.

Faith is not built in a crisis.

It is revealed in one.

And it is built in the ordinary days: in the conversations, the walks, the quiet moments where nobody else is watching.

God makes a promise about this:

Not half your heart.

Not the leftover part of your heart after work, after scrolling, after everything else.

All of it.

And when you do, Jesus extends one of the most beautiful invitations in all of Scripture:

Burned out on religion.

Some of us are not tired of God.

We are tired of trying to earn Him.

But Jesus is not offering you a religion. He is offering you a walk. Walk with Me. Work with Me. Watch how I do it.

That is a relationship.

First Things First

So here is what I want you to take from this chapter.

Put God first.

Not first on a list that you check off in the morning and forget by lunch.

First in your heart, your decisions, your attention, and your love.

Jesus said:

Your Father knows what you need.

He is not unaware of the job you are praying for. The healing. The relationship. The breakthrough. The door.

He knows.

And His instruction is simple.

Seek Him first.

And all these things will be given to you as well.

Notice the order. It does not say, “Seek these things, and God as well.” It says, “Seek God, and these things as well.”

When God is first, everything else finds its place.

When anything else is first, even good things become heavy.

David said it like this:

And something beautiful happens when you delight in Him. Your desires start to change. You begin to want what He wants. And what He wants for you is always better than what you would have asked for on your own.

David had one priority above all others:

One thing.

Not a palace, a victory, or a bigger kingdom.

Him.

So what does putting God first look like in real life? Here are a few places to start.

  • Talk to Him before you talk to anyone else. Before you check your phone in the morning, say good morning to the One who gave you the morning.
  • Bring Him the small things. Do not save prayer for emergencies. Friends talk about everyday life.
  • Listen, not just ask. Sit at His feet like Mary. Open His word and let Him speak first.
  • Hold your blessings loosely. Thank Him for every gift, and remind your heart often that He is better than all of them.
  • Come home quickly. When you drift, and we all do, do not stay away out of shame. Remember the father watching the road. Turn around. He is already running toward you.

He Is Still Asking

The Bible begins with God walking in a garden, looking for the people He made.

And it ends with God dwelling with His people forever.

Everything in between is the story of a God who refused to stop looking.

He walked with Adam and spoke to Moses as a friend. He sent prophets, and then His Son. Even now, He stands at the door and knocks.

All of it for one reason.

He wants you.

Not just your worship, your service, or your Sunday.

You.

So let me ask you the question God asked in the garden.

Where are you?

Not where you live. Not what you do. Not how busy you are.

Where is your heart in relation to Him?

Maybe you have drifted in a good season and did not even notice. Or you have been so busy serving Him that you forgot to sit with Him. Perhaps you have been angry at Him for saying no to something you really wanted.

Wherever you are, it is not too far.

The God who walked in the garden is still walking toward you.

And the best answer you can ever give Him is the one Abraham gave, the one Moses gave, the one the prophets gave when God called their name.

Come with me to a hillside in Galilee.

Can my little go far? What about my small beginning, my ordinary story, my one talent, my late start, my unfinished education, my simple background?

Jesus takes the boy’s lunch, gives thanks, and starts to break it.

He keeps breaking it, and it keeps going.

Miracle complete. Crowd fed. Wonderful.

Let nothing be wasted.

The miracle was over, and Jesus still cared about the crumbs.

The God who does not waste anything.

Not a single piece.

Now look at what happened next.

Twelve baskets.

Think about that for a moment.

They started with five small loaves.

They finished with twelve full baskets.

The leftovers were bigger than the lunch.

And notice the number.

Twelve baskets.

Twelve disciples.

Every single one of them walked away from that hillside carrying a basket. A personal reminder in their own hands. A souvenir of what God can do with a little, when it is placed in His hands.

I believe God still does that.

He lets you carry something out of every season. A lesson. A skill. A testimony. A strength you did not know you had.

Something you can hold up later and say, “Look what He did with that.”

Every Detail

If there is one verse that captures the heart of this chapter, it is this one:

In all things.

Not some things. Not the good things only. Not just the parts of your story you would put on social media.

All things.

The Message puts the end of that passage beautifully:

Every detail.

God takes every detail and works it in.

God is not a God of waste.

He is a God of weaving.

He takes threads you thought were random and weaves them into something you could never have designed on your own.

What Is in Your Hand?

Let me show you how God does it.

Moses spent forty years in the desert of Midian looking after sheep.

Forty years.

He had grown up in a palace and been educated in Egypt. Now his days were spent walking through wilderness, finding water and pasture, learning every path and every hill.

To anyone watching, it might have looked like a career that went nowhere.

But God was not wasting a single day.

Because one day, Moses would lead an entire nation through that very same kind of wilderness. And who better to lead millions through the desert than a man who had spent forty years learning it?

Then, at the burning bush, God asked Moses a simple question.

A staff.

An ordinary shepherd’s stick. The tool of his everyday work.

And that same staff would be lifted over the Red Sea.

God did not give Moses something new.

So let me ask you the same question.

What is in your hand?

Your experience, your personality, your skills. The things you learned in jobs you have already left. The languages you speak, how you talk to people, and the details you notice that others miss.

You may think it is ordinary.

In God’s hands, it is a staff.

The walls of Jerusalem had been broken down for years. The gates had been burned. What was once a strong, proud city was now surrounded by heaps of rubble.

Not everyone was impressed. A man named Sanballat stood by and mocked them:

Burned as they are.

Sanballat looked at the rubble and saw rubbish.

God looked at the same rubble and saw a wall.

Fifty two days.

Walls that had lain in ruins for years, rebuilt in less than two months, out of stones people said were finished.

Those burned stones were not thrown away. They became part of the wall.

Every Chapter Counts

Joseph is one of the best examples in all of Scripture of a life where nothing was wasted.

He had dreams as a young man. Then his life took turn after turn he never planned. A coat. A journey to Egypt. A household. A prison. A palace.

And at every stop, Joseph was learning. Managing. Serving. Growing. Interpreting dreams. Building trust with powerful people.

When he finally stood at the top, second only to Pharaoh, every single chapter had prepared him for that moment.

Years later, standing before his brothers, Joseph summed it all up in one sentence:

God intended it for good.

Every chapter counted.

Paul is another one.

Before he met Jesus, Paul was one of the most educated religious scholars of his day. He knew the Scriptures inside out, held Roman citizenship, and made tents by trade.

After he met Jesus, God used all of it.

His education helped him explain the gospel to scholars. His Roman citizenship opened doors and protected him on his journeys. His tentmaking paid his way so he could preach for free. Even his zeal, once pointed in the wrong direction, became the fire that carried the good news across the world.

God did not throw away Paul’s past.

He redeemed it and put it to work.

Nothing about who you have been is beyond God’s ability to use.

Here is a detail that I find so beautiful, and so few people notice it.

On the night Jesus was arrested, Peter stood outside in the cold, warming himself by a fire. It was a hard night for Peter. A night he would have loved to forget.

Another fire.

Jesus did not avoid the memory.

He redeemed it.

He lit a new fire, and around that fire He gave Peter a fresh start. Three times He asked Peter, “Do you love me?” And three times Peter said yes. And each time, Jesus gave him a mission.

God can take any memory and light a new fire next to it.

God did not waste it.

The Years Come Back

God has a promise for you.

He will repay the years.

Not just the things. The years.

God is able to restore time in a way that only God can. In one season He can make up what you thought you lost in ten, and open doors so fast that you look back and realise He was preparing you all along.

Everything.

In its time.

You are not behind. Every piece of you is being gathered.

So let us gather.

  • Take an inventory. Write down the skills, lessons and experiences you have picked up in every season of your life. You will be surprised how full the list gets.
  • Ask what is in your hand. Look at what you already have, not just what you wish you had. Moses started with a stick.
  • Pick up your burned stones. Name the parts of your story you thought were unusable, and hand them to God. He builds with what others call rubble.
  • Thank God for every chapter. Even the ones you did not choose. Gratitude turns your past into a testimony.
  • Offer it all back to Him. Like the boy with his lunch, place what you have in His hands. That is where little becomes enough.

Get Ready

Here is the most exciting part.

If God has wasted nothing, then everything you have lived through has been preparing you for something.

And that something is still ahead.

See, I am doing a new thing.

Now it springs up.

Not someday. Now.

And look at what God promises to do. Streams in the wasteland.

Paul said it like a man lacing up his running shoes:

I’m off and running.

That is where I want to leave you.

Not looking back with regret.

Looking forward with expectation.

Everything you have been through has been gathered. Every piece. Every season. Every lesson. Every crumb.

Nothing was lost, and nothing was wasted.

And the God who filled twelve baskets from one boy’s lunch is not finished with your story.

So pick up your basket.

Lift your eyes.

Something new is springing up.

Get ready.

Faith That Moves

Things not yet seen.

The Feet Went First

Let me show you one of my favourite moments in the Old Testament.

After forty years in the wilderness, Israel finally reaches the edge of the Promised Land. There is just one problem.

The Jordan River is in the way.

And it is not a calm, shallow stream. It is harvest season, and the river is overflowing its banks.

God gives Joshua the instructions. The priests carrying the ark of the covenant are to walk to the edge of the river, and step in.

Not wait for the river to part.

Step in.

And here is what happened:

Did you catch it?

The feet were dipped in the brim of the water.

Then the waters stood.

The river did not stop so they could step in.

It stopped because they stepped in.

And notice one more detail.

The water stopped “very far” upstream, at a city called Adam. Miles away. Completely out of sight.

The priests could not see what God was doing. All they knew was that their feet were in the water.

But the moment they obeyed, God was already working somewhere they could not see.

Your first step works the same way.

You may not see anything change right away. But the moment you obey, God is already moving upstream.

Because You Say So

Peter learned this lesson on an ordinary morning.

He and his partners had fished all night and caught nothing. Tired, they were washing their nets. They were done.

Then Jesus told Peter to push out into deep water and let the nets down again.

Peter was a professional fisherman. Jesus was a carpenter. On paper, Peter knew better.

But listen to his answer:

Because You say so.

That is faith in five words.

Not because it makes sense, or because I feel like it, or because last night went well.

Because You say so.

The breakthrough was on the other side of “because You say so.”

Healed on the Way

There is a quiet detail in the story of the ten lepers that I love.

When they called out to Jesus for mercy, He did not touch them. He did not pray over them. He simply gave them an instruction.

As they went.

They were still sick when they started walking, on their way to show the priests a healing that had not happened yet.

And somewhere on the road, it happened.

Sometimes God will not show you the answer before you move. He shows it while you are moving.

Through the Crowd

There is a woman in the Gospels whose name we never learn, but whose step of faith has been talked about for two thousand years.

For twelve years, she had been sick. She had spent everything she had on doctors, and instead of getting better, she grew worse. On top of that, her condition made her an outsider, someone who was not supposed to be in crowds or to touch anyone.

But she heard that Jesus was passing through.

Think about what that step required.

She had to leave her house and walk into a crowd she was not supposed to be in. Then she had to push past people, bend down low, and reach out her hand.

No one invited her or cleared a path for her. She just moved.

And the moment she touched Him, she was healed.

Jesus stopped. He turned around. He wanted to know who had touched Him. And when she came forward, trembling, He did not scold her.

Daughter.

She came as an outsider. She left as family.

And Jesus did not say, “My power has healed you,” though it was His power that did it. He said, “Your faith has healed you.”

Her faith was the hand that reached out.

Sometimes your first step will not be a grand leap. It will be a reach. A small, brave movement toward Jesus through a crowd of reasons not to.

That reach matters more than you know.

Too Simple to Obey

Not every first step is dramatic. Sometimes the hardest step is the simple one.

Naaman was a powerful army commander with a skin disease. He travelled to Israel to see the prophet Elisha, expecting something impressive. Instead, Elisha did not even come out to meet him. He just sent a message: go and wash seven times in the Jordan.

Naaman was offended. He almost went home.

But his servants said something wise:

If God had asked for something big, you would have done it.

So why not do the simple thing?

Sometimes the first step God is asking for is not a leap.

It is a phone call. An apology. A prayer. An application. A conversation. A yes.

Small, simple, and easy to ignore.

But obedience is never small to God.

The First Post

I remember the first time I shared something I had written about God.

For a long time, I had been writing short devotionals for myself. A few thoughts on a verse. A lesson God was teaching me. Something I wanted to remember. They lived quietly in my notes, where no one else could see them.

Then I started feeling a nudge to share one.

I resisted it for weeks.

What if people think I am trying to sound holy? Or nobody reads it? Or somebody who knows my past rolls their eyes?

One evening, I finally typed it out, read it over far too many times, and pressed post before I could change my mind. Then I put my phone face down and tried not to think about it.

The next morning, I had a message from someone I had not spoken to in a long time.

They said they had been going through a hard week, and that the words I shared were exactly what they needed to hear that night.

I sat there and read that message again and again.

That person was on the other side of a step I almost did not take.

That is the thing about obedience. It is rarely just about you. When you step out, God often uses that step to reach someone you cannot even see yet.

Light for One Step

One reason we struggle to take the first step is that we want to see the whole path before we start.

God rarely works like that.

A lamp for my feet.

Not a floodlight for the whole road. Just enough light for the next step.

In Bible times, a lamp only lit up a few feet ahead. The end of the road stayed dark. All you could see was where to put your foot next.

And that was enough.

Because every time you take a step, the light moves with you.

God will not always show you the whole staircase.

But He will always light the next step.

What If I Get It Wrong?

There is a fear that keeps a lot of us standing on the shore.

What if I step out and get it wrong?

Maybe I misheard God. I could fail, and people might laugh.

Let me tell you something that has brought me a lot of peace.

God is very good at steering people who are already moving.

Look at Paul. On one of his missionary journeys, he and his team tried to go into the province of Asia to preach. The Holy Spirit stopped them. So they tried to go to Bithynia instead. Again, the door closed. Then one night, Paul had a vision of a man from Macedonia begging him to come and help. And Paul went, and the gospel entered Europe for the first time.

Paul did not get every direction right on the first try.

But he was moving. And God redirected him.

It is much easier to steer a car that is rolling than one that is parked.

And Paul wrote these words to a young man named Timothy, who seems to have struggled with fear:

Timidity does not come from God.

Power does. Love does. A steady, disciplined mind does.

So if you step out and need correcting, God will correct you. If you take a wrong turn, God can turn you around.

But He cannot steer you if you never leave the driveway.

Do Not Despise Small Beginnings

Maybe your first step feels too small to matter.

One page. One prayer. One conversation. One new habit. One Sunday back in church.

God has a word for you:

Small beginnings are not a sign that God is not in it.

They are often the sign that He is.

The river parted for wet feet. The nets filled for one more cast. The lepers were healed on an ordinary road.

Every one of those miracles started with a step that looked small at the time.

When Prayer Becomes Motion

There is a moment in the story of the Exodus that surprises a lot of people.

Israel has just left Egypt. Behind them, Pharaoh’s army is closing in. In front of them is the Red Sea. They are trapped. The people are terrified, and Moses cries out to God.

And God answers him with this:

Why are you crying out to Me?

Move on.

God was not against Moses praying. Moses prayed constantly. But in that moment, the time for asking had passed. God had already given the instruction. Now it was time to move.

There are seasons when we keep praying about something God has already answered. We ask for confirmation, and then confirmation of the confirmation. We wait for one more sign.

And sometimes heaven is gently saying, “I have already told you. Now go.”

Prayer is never wasted. But there comes a moment when the most faithful prayer you can pray is the one you pray with your feet.

Take It

So let me ask you.

What is the first step God has been putting on your heart?

You do not need to see the whole path, feel fearless, or have everything figured out.

You just need to take the next step He is showing you.

  • Name the step. Write it down. Be specific. Not “someday” but “this week.”
  • Make it small enough to start. If the whole thing feels overwhelming, find the first piece. Then do that.
  • Lean on His word. When doubt shows up, say “because You say so,” and let that be your reason.
  • Expect God to move upstream. You may not see it yet, but your obedience starts things in motion far beyond what you can see.
  • Keep walking. Some answers only show up on the way.

The priests did not get to watch the river part from the shore.

They had to step in first.

And so do we.

Take it.

Though It Linger

10 min read13 readings16 passages of Scripture

I used to think waiting was the space between faith and the answer.

You pray. You believe. And then you wait, hands in your lap, until God finally shows up.

But somewhere along the way, I realised I had it backwards.

Waiting is not the gap in your faith. It is where your faith grows up.

Think about the seasons in your own life when you were waiting on God. For a door to open, a prayer to be answered. For clarity, for healing, for a breakthrough, for a “yes.”

Those seasons probably did not feel spiritual. They probably felt slow. Quiet. Maybe even forgotten.

But I have come to believe that some of the most important work God ever does in us happens in exactly those seasons.

The Inbox

Let me tell you about one of my own waiting seasons.

There was a stretch of time, during the job search I told you about in Chapter Three, when my phone became the centre of my day.

I would wake up and check my email before I had even prayed, and refresh my inbox in the middle of conversations. Every time my phone buzzed, my heart jumped, and then sank when it was just another newsletter.

I was waiting, but not in faith.

Anxiety had taken its place.

And one evening, after refreshing my inbox for what must have been the twentieth time that day, I felt the Holy Spirit gently ask me a question.

Who are you really waiting on?

It stopped me.

Because the honest answer was that I was waiting on an email, a recruiter, a hiring manager. On a decision someone else would make.

I had forgotten that I was really waiting on God.

So I made a small change. Every time I felt the urge to refresh my inbox, I would pray instead. Just a short prayer. “Lord, I trust You with this. You know where I am going.”

My circumstances did not change overnight.

But my waiting did.

The anxiety loosened its grip. The days felt less heavy. I was still waiting, but now I was waiting with someone.

It Will Certainly Come

The prophet Habakkuk was waiting too.

He looked at the world around him, at the injustice, the confusion, the silence, and he asked God how long it would take. And God gave him an answer that has carried believers for thousands of years:

Look at the two promises side by side.

“Though it linger.”

“It will certainly come.”

God does not pretend the wait will be short. He admits it may linger.

But He also promises that the lingering is not the end of the story.

It is appointed and certain, and it is coming.

And notice the last phrase: “and will not delay.”

How can something linger and not delay at the same time?

Because from where we stand, it feels slow. But from where God stands, it is right on time.

The Man Who Waited His Whole Life

There is a man in the Gospel of Luke who barely gets a few verses, but his story is one of the most beautiful pictures of faithful waiting in the whole Bible.

His name was Simeon.

Think about what that means.

Simeon received a promise and then had to keep living. Day after day. Year after year. Getting older. Watching people come and go from the temple.

Still waiting.

And then one ordinary day, a young couple walked in carrying a baby.

As you have promised.

God kept His word.

It took a lifetime. But Simeon did not miss it, because he never stopped showing up.

He was in the temple, ready, on the day the promise walked through the door.

Even Jesus Waited

Here is something about Jesus that I think we often overlook.

About thirty years old.

Think about that. The Son of God, the One who came to save the world, spent around thirty years in relative obscurity before His public ministry even began. He grew up in a small town. He worked with His hands. He learned. He waited.

And then His public ministry lasted about three years.

Thirty years of preparation for three years of purpose.

If Jesus did not consider those years wasted, neither should you.

David had a similar story. He was anointed as king while he was still a young shepherd. But he did not take the throne the next day.

Between the anointing and the crown, there were years: serving in Saul’s court, running and hiding in caves, leading a ragged group of outcasts in the wilderness.

The promise was real the day Samuel poured the oil.

But it was being prepared in him for years before it was revealed to everyone else.

Your season of waiting may be doing the same thing. Preparing you to carry what God has already promised.

Hannah Kept Coming Back

Hannah’s story is another one.

She wanted a child more than anything. And year after year, she went to the house of God, prayed, and went home with the same empty arms.

She was mocked and misunderstood. At one point, the priest even thought she was drunk because she was praying so intensely.

But she kept coming back.

And the Bible says this, so simply that it is easy to miss the years inside it:

In the course of time.

Not instantly. Not on her schedule.

But it came.

And the son she waited for became one of the greatest prophets Israel ever had.

The length of her wait was not a sign that God had said no. It was part of preparing something that was bigger than what she had asked for.

A Cloud the Size of a Hand

After three and a half years without rain, the prophet Elijah climbed to the top of Mount Carmel to pray for it.

He prayed, and sent his servant to look out toward the sea. Nothing.

So he prayed again. Still nothing.

Six times, the servant came back with the same report.

There is nothing there.

Then came the seventh time.

A cloud as small as a hand.

That is all.

But Elijah did not say, “That’s it?” He told everyone to get ready, because heavy rain was coming.

And it did.

Here is what I love about this. Elijah knew how to read a small sign. He did not need the whole storm to show up before he believed it was coming.

Sometimes, in your waiting, God will give you a small cloud. A conversation. A door cracking open. A peace you cannot explain.

Do not despise it.

It might be the first sign of the rain.

Wait for the Gift

Before Jesus returned to heaven, He gave His disciples a very specific instruction.

Wait.

They had just seen the risen Jesus and been commissioned to take the gospel to the whole world. Everything in them must have wanted to go.

But Jesus told them to wait first.

And He told them why:

The waiting was not delay.

It was preparation for power.

About a hundred and twenty of them gathered in an upper room and prayed. Ten days later, on the day of Pentecost, the Holy Spirit came. Peter stood up and preached, and about three thousand people believed in a single day.

What happened in that one day could never have happened without the ten days before it.

Faith and Patience

The book of Hebrews puts faith and patience side by side:

Faith and patience.

Faith believes the promise.

Patience stays with it until it arrives.

You need both. Faith without patience gives up too early. Patience without faith just waits for nothing.

James uses the picture of a farmer:

A farmer does not plant a seed on Monday and dig it up on Wednesday to check if it worked. He waters it. He protects it. He trusts the process that he cannot see under the soil.

Waiting in faith is not doing nothing.

It is tending what God has planted.

Waiting Is Not Worrying

There is a difference between waiting and worrying, even though they can feel the same.

Worrying keeps replaying the problem.

Waiting keeps returning to the promise.

Paul gave us a beautiful way to wait well:

Prayer. Petition. Thanksgiving.

Not anxiety.

And David wrote about what happened when he waited this way:

Look at the order.

He waited.

God turned to him and heard him. He lifted him, set his feet on a rock, and gave him a new song.

The waiting came first. Everything else followed.

What to Do While You Wait

So how do we wait well?

Here are a few things that have helped me.

  • Keep showing up. Like Simeon in the temple and Hannah at the house of God, stay in the place where God has met you before.
  • Keep praying, even when it feels repetitive. Elijah prayed seven times. Persistence is not a lack of faith. It is faith refusing to quit.
  • Watch for small clouds. Notice the small signs of God’s movement, and thank Him for them.
  • Keep doing good. “Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up.” (Galatians 6:9)
  • Let God strengthen you now. Not just after the waiting, but in the middle of it.

That last one matters more than we realise.

The strength does not come after the wait.

It comes in it.

The Laughter at the End

I want to end this chapter with a smile, because waiting does not always end in tears.

Sometimes it ends in laughter.

Sarah waited decades for a child. When God finally told Abraham that she would have a son within the year, Sarah was listening from inside the tent. She was well past the age of having children. And she laughed. Not a joyful laugh. A tired, it is too late for that kind of laugh.

But God kept His word.

A year later, Sarah was holding her son. And they named him Isaac, a name that means “he laughs.”

Listen to what Sarah said:

The same woman who laughed in disbelief was now laughing in joy.

God took the very sound of her doubt and turned it into the sound of her celebration.

He can do the same with your waiting.

The season that once made you sigh might one day make you laugh. And the people around you will laugh with you, because they will see what God did.

It Is Coming

Wherever you are waiting right now, I want to say this to you as clearly as I can.

Your waiting is not wasted, and your prayers have not been lost. This season has not been forgotten.

God is not late, not distracted, and not done.

Though it linger, wait for it.

It will certainly come.

Be strong.

Take heart.

And wait, not like someone who has given up, but like someone who knows that the One who promised is faithful.

Keep the Faith

10 min read13 readings12 passages of Scripture

We have come a long way together.

It started with a simple question: what is faith?

Along the way, we found out it is more than belief. It is putting your whole weight on God.

We learned that the biggest limits are often the sentences in our own minds, and that praise is the sound faith makes. God is not just looking for worship, He is looking for us. Nothing in our story is wasted. Faith takes the first step, and faith knows how to wait.

Now we come to the last question.

How do you keep it?

Not for a day, or for a good season. For a lifetime.

The Last Words of Paul

When Paul wrote his final letter, he was in prison in Rome. He knew his life was nearly over. He had been beaten, shipwrecked, betrayed, misunderstood and abandoned by people he trusted.

If anyone had reasons to let go of faith, it was Paul.

And this is what he wrote:

Three phrases.

Fought. Finished. Kept.

Paul did not say, “I never struggled.” He said he fought.

He never claimed it was easy, only that he finished.

And instead of “I always felt strong,” he said he kept the faith.

That is the goal.

Not a perfect life. A faithful one.

Faith Is a Race, Not a Moment

There is a reason the Bible so often describes faith as a race.

A race is not won at the starting line, or by the person who sprints the first hundred metres and then sits down.

The one who keeps going wins it.

A great cloud of witnesses.

That is the chapter right before it: Hebrews 11, the great list of people who lived by faith. Abraham, Moses, Rahab, Gideon, David, and many more.

They have already run their race.

And now it is our turn.

Notice what we are told to do.

Throw off what hinders. Run with perseverance. Fix our eyes on Jesus.

Not on the crowd, the runner beside us, or how far we still have to go.

On Jesus.

By Faith

Hebrews 11 is sometimes called the Hall of Faith. Read it slowly, and you will notice that almost every story begins with the same two words.

By faith.

Abel offered a better sacrifice. Enoch walked with God, Noah built an ark, and Abraham went, even though he did not know where he was going. Sarah, past childbearing age, received the promise. Moses left Egypt, not fearing the king’s anger. The people passed through the Red Sea as on dry land, the walls of Jericho fell, and Rahab, an outsider, was welcomed among God’s people.

And then the writer says, in effect, “I do not have time to tell you about Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel and the prophets.”

Here is what strikes me about that list.

These were not perfect people. Abraham lied about his wife. Sarah laughed at God’s promise. Moses killed a man. Gideon hid in a winepress. Samson made terrible choices. David failed badly.

Yet they are remembered by two words.

By faith.

Not by their mistakes or their worst moments.

By their faith.

One day, the story of your life will be told too. I pray that the two words written over it will be the same.

By faith.

He Started It, He Will Finish It

Here is the part that takes the pressure off.

Jesus is called “the pioneer and perfecter of faith.”

The pioneer is the one who starts something.

The perfecter is the one who completes it.

Your faith did not start with you. And it does not depend on you alone to finish.

Paul said it like this to the church in Philippi:

He who began it will complete it.

Keeping the faith is not about gripping so hard that you never slip.

It is about staying close to the One who never lets go.

When Faith Feels Weak

There will be days when your faith feels small.

Days when prayer feels like talking to the ceiling. When the waiting drags on. When you wonder if you heard God right at all.

Jesus knew those days would come.

On the night before the cross, He told Peter something remarkable. Peter was about to fail badly. Jesus knew it. And instead of rejecting him, He said this:

Jesus prayed for Peter’s faith.

And Hebrews tells us He is still praying for us today.

So on the days when your faith feels weak, remember this. The strength of your faith does not rest only on how strong you feel.

It rests on the One who is praying for you.

Even If He Does Not

There is a kind of faith that I believe is the deepest of all.

It is the faith of three young men named Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego.

The king of Babylon had set up a giant golden statue and commanded everyone to bow down to it. Anyone who refused would be thrown into a blazing furnace. These three refused.

Standing before the king, with the furnace roaring behind them, they said this:

Even if He does not.

Those four words are the heart of keeping the faith.

They believed God could save them, and that He would. But their faith did not depend on it.

Whether God rescued them from the fire or not, they had already decided who they belonged to.

Job said something similar in the middle of his suffering:

This is not a faith that only holds on when things go our way.

It is a faith that holds on, period.

And here is the beautiful part of the story. God did deliver Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego. When the king looked into the furnace, he saw not three men but four, walking around unbound and unharmed. And the fourth looked like a son of the gods.

They did not avoid the fire.

But they did not walk through it alone.

Keeping the faith does not mean you will never face the fire. It means that when you do, you will find Someone in there with you.

Faith That Outlives You

There is one more thing about faith I want to leave with you.

Faith is not only something you keep.

It is something you pass on.

Paul once wrote to a young man named Timothy:

A grandmother, a mother, a son.

Faith passed from one generation to the next.

When I read that verse, I think about my own mother. About her words to me on the day I got that rejection email. “Your first reaction should be praise.” She did not just tell me what to believe. She showed me how to live it.

Somebody’s faith is going to be shaped by yours.

A child. A friend. A coworker. Someone who reads your life before they ever read the Bible.

Hebrews 11 says something about those great heroes of faith that always moves me:

Still living by faith.

Right to the end.

Stones to Remember

Do you remember the river from Chapter Six? The Jordan that stopped when the priests stepped in?

There is a part of that story we did not talk about.

After all of Israel had crossed over on dry ground, God told Joshua to have twelve men go back into the riverbed and each pick up a stone. They carried those stones to the place where they camped and stacked them into a memorial.

Why?

The stones were for remembering.

God knew that one day, there would be another challenge. Another season of waiting. Another moment when faith felt weak. And He wanted His people to have something they could point to and say, “Remember what God did here.”

I want to encourage you to collect your own stones.

Write down the prayers God has answered. Keep a journal of the times He came through. Tell your children, your friends, your church, what He has done.

Because keeping the faith is so much easier when you can see the stones.

Writing the Last Chapter

I want to tell you something personal.

As I write these last pages, I am thinking about where this book began. A few lines in the notes on my phone. A voice asking, “Who are you to write a book?” A blank page I was afraid to start.

And now here we are, at the final chapter.

This book is one of my stones.

Every chapter in it represents something God has taught me. The difference between belief and trust. The sentences I had to stop agreeing with. The day my mom told me to make praise my first reaction. The Sunday I realised I had drifted in a good season. The years I thought were wasted that God had been gathering all along. The first step I almost did not take. The waiting that grew roots in me.

I did not write this book because I have finished my race.

I wrote it because I am still running. And I wanted you to know you are not running alone.

Before You Go

So here is my encouragement to you as we close this book.

  • Keep showing up. On the good days and the hard days. Faith grows in the ordinary moments.
  • Keep your eyes on Jesus. Not on the waves, not on the crowd, not on how far you still have to go.
  • Keep good people close. Stay near those who strengthen your faith, and become that person for someone else.
  • Keep coming back. When you drift, and we all do, do not stay away. The Father is always watching the road.
  • Keep the end in mind. One day, you will want to be able to say what Paul said: I kept the faith.

Well Done

Jesus told a story about a master who gave his servants something to look after while he was away. When he came back, he called them in one by one.

To the servant who had been faithful, he said this:

Well done.

Not “well planned,” “well known,” or “well liked.”

Well done, good and faithful.

That is what I want to hear one day. And I believe it is what you want to hear too.

Jesus also gave a promise to a church that was facing hard times:

Faithfulness is not glamorous. Most of it happens in ordinary days that no one else sees. Showing up. Praying again. Trusting again. Choosing God again.

But heaven sees every one of those days.

And one day, it will all be worth it.

Beyond the Waters

When I started writing this book, I did not have everything figured out. I still do not.

But I know this.

The God who met me in the waiting, in the rejection, in the questions, and in the quiet has never once let go of me.

And He will not let go of you.

Whatever water is in front of you right now, a decision, a delay, a disappointment, a dream, it is not bigger than Him.

You can trust Him in it, step through it, and keep the faith beyond it.

I will leave you with one of the oldest blessings in Scripture, the words God Himself gave the priests to speak over His people:

Thank you for walking with me.

Now go.

Keep the faith.

The End

Thank you for walking with me.

If this book encouraged you, share it with someone who is standing at the edge of their own waters. It is free, and it is for them too.
